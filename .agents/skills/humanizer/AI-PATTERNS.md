# AI writing patterns

Use this secondary checklist when prose still feels generic after matching the writer's voice, or when the user explicitly asks for an AI-pattern audit. Do not treat any single pattern as proof that text was AI-generated.

The checklist is based on Wikipedia's ["Signs of AI writing"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing), maintained by WikiProject AI Cleanup.

## Content

1. **Inflated importance:** Ordinary facts are framed as pivotal, enduring, profound, or historically significant.
2. **Name-dropping:** Publications, experts, or follower counts are listed to imply importance without explaining their relevance.
3. **Shallow analysis:** An `-ing` phrase claims a fact is highlighting, reflecting, ensuring, or symbolizing something larger.
4. **Sales language:** Prose uses words such as vibrant, renowned, groundbreaking, breathtaking, or must-visit.
5. **Vague sources:** Claims are assigned to unnamed experts, critics, observers, reports, or publications.
6. **Formulaic outlooks:** Generic challenges, future outlook, or continued-growth sections repeat earlier claims.

## Language and grammar

7. **Stock AI words:** Watch for clusters of words such as crucial, delve, enhance, foster, highlight, intricate, pivotal, showcase, tapestry, underscore, and valuable.
8. **Avoiding simple verbs:** `Is`, `are`, and `has` are replaced by serves as, stands as, represents, boasts, features, or offers.
9. **Not X but Y:** The prose repeatedly uses "not only," "not just," or "not merely" to manufacture contrast.
10. **Forced groups of three:** Ideas are padded or grouped into threes to sound complete.
11. **Mechanical repetition fixes:** The same subject is renamed repeatedly, or several sentences begin identically without purpose.
12. **False ranges:** "From X to Y" joins examples that do not form a real range.
13. **Passive voice and missing subjects:** The prose hides who acts or drops the subject when naming it would be clearer.

## Style

14. **Dash overuse:** Em dashes, en dashes, or double hyphens interrupt too many sentences. Follow the writer's sample rather than banning them.
15. **Excessive bolding:** Ordinary terms are bolded for emphasis.
16. **Bold mini-headings:** Every list item begins with a bold label and colon.
17. **Title-case headings:** Every major word in a heading is capitalized without a style reason.
18. **Decorative emojis:** Emojis are added to headings or bullets without carrying meaning.
19. **Mismatched quotation marks:** Curly or straight quotes do not match the writer or target format.

## Chatbot artifacts

20. **Chatbot framing:** Greetings, praise, offers to continue, and closings such as "I hope this helps" remain in standalone prose.
21. **Knowledge disclaimers and guesses:** Missing information is padded with cutoff disclaimers or plausible but unsupported details.
22. **Over-agreement:** The response praises or validates the user before making its point.

## Filler and hedging

23. **Filler phrases:** Prefer direct forms such as "to," "because," "now," "if," and "can."
24. **Stacked qualifiers:** Several caveats weaken one claim. Keep only the uncertainty the meaning requires.
25. **Generic positive endings:** The piece ends with vague optimism instead of its last concrete point.
26. **Unnecessary hyphenation:** Compound adjectives remain hyphenated when used after the noun.
27. **Fake depth:** Phrases such as "the real question," "at its core," and "what really matters" inflate an ordinary point.
28. **Announcing the next point:** "Let's dive in" or "here's what you need to know" appears instead of the point itself.
29. **Repeating the heading:** The first sentence merely restates the section heading.
30. **Describing the old draft:** Current documentation explains what it replaced instead of how it works now.
31. **Forced punchlines:** Several short fragments turn every sentence into a dramatic reveal.
32. **Formulaic sayings:** The prose uses constructions such as "X is the Y of Z" instead of making the specific claim.
33. **Fake-candid openings:** "Honestly," "look," or "here's the thing" stages intimacy before an ordinary point.
34. **Unraised objections:** The text denies claims nobody made instead of stating its argument directly.
35. **Fake alternatives:** An implausible option is introduced only to be dismissed.

## False positives

Do not flag a pattern in isolation. In particular, preserve these when they match the writer:

- Polished grammar, formal words, and common transitions.
- A deliberate repeated opening or one short sentence for emphasis.
- Em dashes, curly quotes, asides, and parentheticals.
- Real objections, alternatives, limits, corrections, and disclaimers.
- Specific details, mixed feelings, humor, uncertainty, slang, and uneven rhythm.
- Quotations, titles, proper names, and secondhand text.

When several stock patterns occur together, rewrite the paragraph around its main point rather than replacing individual words mechanically.
