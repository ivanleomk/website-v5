Token anxiety is a real thing. It is easy to look at people bragging about burning billions of tokens, juggling seven Codex accounts, or spinning up swarms of autonomous agents, and feel like you are falling behind.

That is starting with the wrong metric. Token maxxing and value maxxing are just different sides of the same coin. Burning tokens is only the byproduct of running a model; the actual result you want is driving something economically viable for you or someone else.

Instead of measuring what you use relative to someone else, start with your own day-to-day blockers. Working with AI is a competency you build over time. For the first time in human history, we have intelligence on tap. Like any skill, you do not start at the peak. You experiment, build small competencies, and let that snowball into compounding leverage.

## Start Simple

When people try to use AI, they often hunt for massive, world-changing use cases on day one: building an automated software factory or automating an entire department. When that breaks, they assume models are not ready.

Instead of searching for a giant project, start with the small, everyday frictions right in front of you:

- Finding a restaurant that meets tricky dietary constraints in an unfamiliar neighborhood
- Looking up citations, specific codons, or chemical properties during scientific research
- Reorganizing cluttered folders and messy downloads on your desktop

These examples sound like cliché product demos, but they give you immediate wins. If you are doing scientific research, for example, using Antigravity alongside Google DeepMind's [Science Skills](https://github.com/google-deepmind/science-skills) lets you automate repetitive literature lookups, data extraction, and citation tracing across databases like AlphaGenome and UniProt. When the model handles the initial retrieval across hundreds of papers, your primary job shifts from manual searching to verifying citations and evaluating results.

Starting with focused goals builds momentum and confidence in your competence. Once you see how models behave on real inputs, combining a few small wins lets you tackle far larger tasks.

## Iteration as a Skill

Working with models is no different from acquiring normal skills like riding a bike, lifting weights, learning a language, or improving at sales:

1. You define an ideal outcome
2. You take an action
3. You get immediate feedback
4. You adjust and try again

Learning to leverage models just means understanding how to iterate quickly. You do not need a perfect solution or a complex multi-agent framework to start. You prompt the model, see where it fails, give it better context, and adjust.

While that might feel vibes-based at first, the core is being curious and experimenting. If you do not actively prompt and test models, you never develop an intuitive sense of what works. Every prompt and evaluation gives you direct signal on what the model can handle and where it needs guidance.

## Seeing What Running Looks Like

People often say that you need to learn to walk before you can run.

With models, that dynamic flips: they let you see what running looks like right away. You can one-shot ambitious applications that would have previously required weeks of grinding through boilerplate. Once you see what running looks like, your intuition for what is possible changes.

Over time, models stop being just a way to shave five minutes off a chore and expand what you can do.

For example, I have been learning Korean over the past few months. The biggest shift for me was having Grokbot message me vocabulary and short reading passages every morning. From a quick text on the go, it can generate personalized passages, build flashcard web apps, and quiz me on tricky concepts. Paired with Gemini text-to-speech, I can turn what I am reading into custom audio lessons and podcasts tailored to my comprehension level. A big part of this is simply communicating to the model what "good" looks like for your specific workflow.

We now have models that can hold and reason across massive contexts, tackling benchmarks like Artificial Analysis's Briefcase that evaluate tasks spanning 80 different files at once. When models can handle that much context, execution speed is no longer the bottleneck. Your imagination and your curiosity set the ceiling.

## Knowledge as an Architecture

A lot of writing online fixates on loop engineering, harness engineering, and micro-optimizing individual agent workflows.

The better way to think about knowledge is as an architecture you build over time. It is not a single tower with one peak, but an interconnected structure spanning your different interests, the things you are curious about, the work you need to finish, and parts of your life you never previously bothered to optimize.

You do not even need to be locked into a single subscription interface when you can grab an API key from OpenRouter and explore freely. If you can increase your inspiration, eliminate repetitive chores you dislike, and make sense of the raw context in your daily life, there is suddenly so much more you can dream up and execute.

## Cultivating Taste and Verification

As your ambition grows, the differentiator becomes taste: developing strong opinions and a clear standard for what the final output should look like.

Traditionally, a software engineer developed taste by spending years on the job, reading thousands of pull requests, and grinding through dense documentation. With language models acting as infinitely patient teachers, code generation is cheap, so your primary job becomes verification.

Before I worked on LLMs and joined DeepMind, I built a structured outputs parser compatible with Hugging Face's Transformers library so that passing a Pydantic model returned structured JSON. The model generated the initial code in about 10 minutes. But I spent the next two hours studying every line it wrote, inspecting edge cases, and understanding the token masking mechanics.

In the past, understanding that architecture would have required days of digging through obscure tutorials and codebases. With a model, you can ask every naive question on your mind and build deep intuition at the speed of your curiosity.

## Scaling Leverage to Others

Start optimizing yourself out of the loop so you can focus on higher-leverage problems.

In an organization, that means figuring out how to unblock others and help them solve problems. Think of it like cooking a meal: you do not wait for the vegetables to finish boiling before you start stir-frying the meat. You identify what can run asynchronously, get the model working in parallel, and keep multiple burners going.

When you run agents or test software, orient the outputs toward artifacts that save your team time:

- Writing detailed friction logs that document the exact user experience and failure points
- Drafting reproducible test cases
- Filing precise bug reports with clear reproduction steps
- Turning raw PR discussions and meeting notes into searchable internal documentation

The mindset shifts from "How do I use AI?" to "What are my actual goals, and how do I unblock myself and others?"

## Conclusion

Token maxxing and value maxxing are just labels for using a tool. It is not a contest to see who burns the most compute, stacks the most subscriptions, or builds the most elaborate multi-agent harness.

The real unlock is much simpler:

1. **Start with your daily friction.** Pick the small, annoying blocker right in front of you and get an immediate win.
2. **Treat iteration as a physical skill.** Define the outcome, prompt the model, inspect where it breaks, and adjust.
3. **Use models as patient teachers.** Cheap generation frees you to focus on verification, taste, and building intuition.
4. **Unblock yourself and others.** Keep multiple burners going in parallel and turn model runs into high-leverage artifacts.

Token anxiety fades when you stop measuring token volume and start measuring the value of what you unblock. Start with what you are curious about, build the habit bit by bit, and let the compounding take care of the rest.
